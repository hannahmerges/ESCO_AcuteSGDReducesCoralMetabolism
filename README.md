# Acute exposure to submarine groundwater discharge reduces metabolism in a nearshore tropical coral, Pocillopora acuta

Freshwater inputs to coastal marine ecosystems can introduce biogeochemical conditions that differ markedly from ambient seawater. This distinct biogeochemistry can affect important physiological processes to the organisms that inhabit these ecosystems. A common source of freshwater to coastal marine ecosystems, like coral reefs, is submarine groundwater discharge (SGD). SGD is nutrient-rich, fresh or brackish water with low pH that enters the ocean from terrestrial zones by passing through aquifers or the marginal seabed. Because SGD fluxes are changing globally, the biogeochemical composition and the prevalence of SGD may intensify in coastal ecosystems, which can have varying impacts on the constituents of those ecosystems. Using a series of SGD dilutions (0-4% SGD by volume) we analyzed the effect of SGD-driven biogeochemistry (nitrate + nitrite, phosphate, silicate, and pH) on the metabolism of a dominant tropical fringing reef coral, Pocillopora acuta. This study occurred from May to June, 2023 on the west side of Moorea, French Polynesia, and focused on groundwater from two SGD sources with unique biogeochemical conditions. Our findings highlight metabolic depression in respiration, gross photosynthesis, and calcification in response to increasing SGD influence. Respiration and gross photosynthesis were driven predominantly by SGD-driven nutrients, where both rates had a negative relationship with nitrate + nitrite. Calcification was most affected by SGD-driven pH, with SGD-driven acidification reducing coral calcification. Together, these findings demonstrate that SGD-driven changes in coastal biogeochemistry can simultaneously alter multiple components of coral metabolism, highlighting an important pathway through which changing land–sea connections may alter the functioning of nearshore coral reef ecosystems.
